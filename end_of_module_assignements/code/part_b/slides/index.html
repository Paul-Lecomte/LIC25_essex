Technical Overview of AI

  • Definition: simulation of human intelligence in machines (Floridi et al., 2018)
  • Subfields: machine learning, deep learning, natural language processing
  • Core components: algorithms, data, computing infrastructure
  • Current state: large language models, generative AI, multimodal systems
  • 84+ national AI ethics guidelines converge on shared principles (Jobin et al., 2019)
AI refers to machines performing tasks associated with human intelligence: machine learning, deep learning, natural language processing and, most recently, large language models. Floridi and colleagues describe AI as a general-purpose technology whose defining feature is optimising goals across contexts, while Jobin and colleagues show that over eighty-four national and international ethics guidelines now converge on shared principles such as transparency, fairness and accountability (Floridi et al., 2018; Jobin et al., 2019). The technical story is mature technology meeting rapidly evolving norms.

Ethical Concerns in AI (with case studies)

  • Bias and discrimination: data bias, algorithmic bias, disparate impact
  • Transparency: black-box problem; GDPR 'right to explanation' (Goodman and Flaxman, 2017)
  • Fairness is a political-philosophical choice, not just a metric (Binns, 2018)
  • End-to-end algorithmic auditing closes the accountability gap (Raji et al., 2020)
Case study 1 - COMPAS: audit found higher false-positive rates for Black defendants; a statistically accurate system can still discriminate (Angwin et al., 2016)
Case study 2 - Amazon recruiting: AI recruiting tool scrapped after downgrading women's CVs learned from male-dominated history (Dastin, 2018)
The central risks are bias, opacity and accountability, and two real cases anchor this. First, ProPublica's audit of the COMPAS recidivism tool found that Black defendants were far more likely to be wrongly flagged as high risk, showing that a statistically accurate system can still produce discriminatory outcomes (Angwin et al., 2016). Second, Amazon scrapped an AI recruiting tool after it systematically downgraded women's CVs because it learned from historically male-dominated hiring data (Dastin, 2018). Binns reminds us that fairness is ultimately a political-philosophical choice rather than a purely mathematical metric, and Goodman and Flaxman link explainability directly to the GDPR right to explanation (Binns, 2018; Goodman and Flaxman, 2017).

Security Risks in AI Systems

  • Adversarial attacks: small input perturbations cause misclassification (Papernot et al., 2016)
  • Model extraction: stealing proprietary models via API queries
  • Privacy attacks: neural networks memorise training data (Carlini et al., 2019)
  • AI-powered threats: enhanced phishing, deepfakes, automated hacking (Raleigh, 2020)
Case study 3 - Microsoft Tay: data poisoning in practice - chatbot manipulated into offensive output within 24 hours of deployment (Vincent, 2016)
AI introduces novel attack surfaces. Papernot and colleagues demonstrated that imperceptible input perturbations can force misclassified predictions - adversarial examples that defeat otherwise robust classifiers (Papernot et al., 2016). Data poisoning is not theoretical: Microsoft's Tay chatbot was manipulated into producing offensive output within twenty-four hours of deployment (Vincent, 2016). Carlini and colleagues showed that neural networks can unintentionally memorise and later reveal training data, and Raleigh documents how AI lowers the barrier for phishing and automated attacks (Carlini et al., 2019; Raleigh, 2020).

EU AI Act risk tiers (European Commission, 2021)

GDPR already constrains automated decision-making in the EU, and the EU AI Act adds a risk-based classification, from unacceptable through high to minimal risk (European Commission, 2021). NIST's AI Risk Management Framework provides a voluntary cycle of Govern, Map, Measure and Manage, while OECD principles offer intergovernmental alignment (NIST, 2023; OECD, 2019). IEEE and ISO standards fill the engineering layer. The remaining challenge is fragmentation: multinationals must comply with several overlapping regimes.

Recommendations, Conclusion & References

Balancing innovation with responsibility:

  • Ethics-by-design across the AI lifecycle
  • Robust testing: adversarial, bias and performance validation
  • Meaningful human oversight for consequential decisions
  • Transparency about capabilities, limitations and data use
  • Continuous monitoring for drift and emergent behaviours
  • Adaptive governance that evolves with understanding

Conclusion: AI augments - does not replace - human expertise and professional judgement
