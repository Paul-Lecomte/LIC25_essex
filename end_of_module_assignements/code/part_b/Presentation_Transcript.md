Floridi and colleagues describe AI as a general-purpose technology whose defining feature is optimising goals across contexts, while Jobin and colleagues show that over eighty-four national and international ethics guidelines now converge on shared principles such as transparency, fairness and accountability (Floridi et al., 2018; Jobin et al., 2019). **Slide 3 - Enterprise applications.** Adoption is already widespread: medical imaging and drug discovery in healthcare, fraud detection and credit scoring in finance, predictive maintenance in manufacturing, route optimisation in transport, and anomaly detection in cybersecurity. **Slide 4 - Ethical concerns.** The central risks are bias, opacity and accountability, and two real cases anchor this. First, ProPublica's audit of the COMPAS recidivism tool found that Black defendants were far more likely to be wrongly flagged as high risk, showing that a statistically accurate system can still produce discriminatory outcomes (Angwin et al., 2016). Second, Amazon scrapped an AI recruiting tool after it systematically downgraded women's CVs because it learned from historically male-dominated hiring data (Dastin, 2018). Binns reminds us that fairness is ultimately a political-philosophical choice rather than a purely mathematical metric, and Goodman and Flaxman link explainability directly to the GDPR right to explanation (Binns, 2018; Goodman and Flaxman, 2017).

**Slide 5 - Security risks.** AI introduces novel attack surfaces. Papernot and colleagues demonstrated that imperceptible input perturbations can force misclassified predictions, defeating otherwise robust classifiers (Papernot et al., 2016). Data poisoning is not theoretical: Microsoft's Tay chatbot was manipulated into producing offensive output within twenty-four hours of deployment (Vincent, 2016). Carlini and colleagues showed that neural networks can unintentionally memorise and later reveal training data, and Raleigh documents how AI lowers the barrier to phishing and automated attacks (Carlini et al., 2019; Raleigh, 2020).

**Slide 6 - Regulation and governance.** Enterprises face a converging framework landscape. GDPR already constrains automated decision-making in the EU, and the EU AI Act adds a risk-based classification from unacceptable through high to minimal risk (European Commission, 2021). NIST's AI Risk Management Framework provides a voluntary cycle of Govern, Map, Measure and Manage, while OECD principles offer intergovernmental alignment (NIST, 2023; OECD, 2019). IEEE and ISO standards fill the engineering layer. The remaining challenge is fragmentation: multinationals must comply with several overlapping regimes.
